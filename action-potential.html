What makes an action potential?

The one-sentence version. An action potential is what happens when channels whose gates respond to voltage are added to the resting membrane: a small push past threshold opens Na⁺ channels, Na⁺ inflow opens more, the voltage shoots toward ENa, and then slower changes, Na⁺ channels plugging themselves and K⁺ channels opening, drag it back to rest and briefly below.

Gates that listen to the voltage

Leak channels are always open. The channels that make a spike have gates that swing with the membrane voltage. A voltage-gated Na⁺ channel has two: a fast activation gate that opens when the membrane depolarizes, and a slower inactivation plug that swings into the pore shortly after. A voltage-gated K⁺ channel has one gate that also opens on depolarization, but slowly. Those three speeds, fast open, slower plug, slow K⁺, are the whole story.

Threshold is where Na⁺ inflow starts to win

A weak stimulus lifts Vm a little, a few Na⁺ gates open, but the K⁺ and leak pulls are stronger and Vm slides back. Push a little harder and enough Na⁺ channels open that the Na⁺ inflow itself depolarizes the membrane, which opens more Na⁺ channels. Once that loop closes, the stimulus no longer matters: the spike runs on its own, which is why it is all-or-none.

Why the peak stops short of ENa

At the top, Na⁺ has most of the votes and Vm heads for ENa. It never arrives, because two slower things catch up: the inactivation plugs close the Na⁺ channels, and the K⁺ gates open. K⁺, now with the most votes and far from its E, pulls Vm back down.

Undershoot and recovery

The K⁺ gates are slow to close, so for a few milliseconds the membrane is more permeable to K⁺ than at rest and Vm dips toward EK. Meanwhile the Na⁺ plugs clear only after the membrane has repolarized. Until they do, a new stimulus cannot fire a full spike: the refractory period. That is what keeps spikes from running backwards along an axon.

Common mix-ups. The pump does not repolarize the membrane; K⁺ leaving through open K⁺ channels does that, within a millisecond. And only a tiny fraction of the ions cross during a spike, so the concentrations do not change; a neuron can fire thousands of times before the pump’s work is noticeable.

Two toxins, one lesson each

Tetrodotoxin (TTX), from pufferfish, plugs voltage-gated Na⁺ channels: stimulate, and all you get is a passive bump. Tetraethylammonium (TEA) blocks voltage-gated K⁺ channels: the spike still fires, because Na⁺ makes the upstroke, but it lasts far longer, because repolarization is left to Na⁺ inactivation and the leak. Together they show which ion does which job.

Words you will see

Voltage-gated channel
A channel whose gates open or close depending on the membrane voltage.
Activation and inactivation
Two different gates on the Na⁺ channel. Activation opens it fast on depolarization; inactivation plugs it a moment later and clears only after repolarization.
Threshold
The depolarization at which Na⁺ inflow starts to outrun the K⁺ and leak outflow, so the spike becomes self-sustaining.
Depolarize, repolarize, hyperpolarize
Move Vm toward zero, back toward rest, or beyond rest to more negative values.
Overshoot and undershoot
The part of the spike above 0 mV, and the dip below rest afterwards.
Refractory period
The time after a spike when another cannot be fired at all (absolute) or only by a stronger stimulus (relative).
All-or-none
Below threshold nothing; above it a full-sized spike, the same size whatever the stimulus.
Hodgkin–Huxley model
The 1952 equations, fitted to squid axon, that describe the gates and reproduce the spike. This page runs them.

Try this in class

  1. Set the strength to 10 and stimulate. Watch Vm bump and slide back. Try 15, then 22. What changes at threshold?
  2. At ×4000, watch one spike and name the order of events: which gate opens first, which closes first?
  3. Why does the peak stop short of ENa (+61 mV)?
  4. Why does Vm dip below rest before coming back?
  5. Turn the strength up to 40. Is the spike any bigger?
  6. Block Na⁺ with TTX and stimulate. What is left? Block K⁺ with TEA instead. Why does the spike last so long, and what brings it down at all?
  7. Show more, then fire two pulses 4 ms apart, then 8, then 15. When does the second spike appear? Which gate sets the limit?
  8. Set Vm to −40 mV and press Hold. Watch the conductance trace: Na⁺ first and brief, K⁺ later and lasting. This is the voltage-clamp experiment Hodgkin and Huxley did.

About the model. The membrane runs the Hodgkin–Huxley equations with their original squid-axon channel densities and gate kinetics, sped up 2× toward body temperature, and with this series’ equilibrium potentials (ENa +61 mV, EK −89 mV, leak −70 mV). Threshold, the spike shape, the undershoot and the refractory period are not scripted; they emerge from the equations. The gates drawn on each channel show the average state of that channel type. Ions on screen are a small sample of the traffic, and the charge marks are cartoon-scaled. Part 3 of the NeuroSim series, after Nernst Tug-of-War and Resting Potential Tug-of-War.
