What is actually pushing the ion?

The one-sentence version. The equilibrium potential is the membrane voltage at which an ion’s electrical pull exactly cancels its diffusion push, so the ion stops moving on net even though its channel is wide open.

Nothing in the channel pushes. A channel is a water-filled pore with a filter that admits one kind of ion and turns the others away. It decides which ion gets to respond to the forces, not how hard they push. Two things push.

Diffusion

No single ion is “pushed” by diffusion. Each one just jitters with thermal energy. But more ions bump into the pore on the crowded side than on the sparse side, so more get through from that side. The concentration gradient is a statistical push, and its strength grows with the ratio of the two concentrations, which is why a logarithm appears in the Nernst equation.

The electric field

Once a few K⁺ have left, the inside is short of positive charge and the outside has a surplus. That unbalanced charge does not spread through the solutions; it lines the two faces of the membrane, which behaves as a capacitor. The charge film creates an electric field across the membrane, and every ion inside the pore feels it as a genuine force: cations are pulled toward the negative face, anions toward the positive face. Both bulk solutions stay electrically neutral.

Why the voltage stops changing

Each K⁺ that leaves makes the inside a little more negative and the pull back inward a little stronger. The diffusion push hardly changes, because the concentrations hardly change. The voltage settles where the two pushes cancel: the equilibrium potential. Nothing has been used up. The gradient is still intact, which is why EK depends only on the concentration ratio and the temperature, not on how many ions moved.

Common mix-up. “Equilibrium” here does not mean the concentrations even out. They stay lopsided, 140 mM inside against 5 mM outside for K⁺. What becomes equal is the size of the two pushes.

Equilibrium is traffic, not stillness

At EK, K⁺ still crosses in both directions, just at equal rates. Watch the crossing counters: outward and inward keep climbing together while the net stops.

Why selectivity matters

Only the permeant ion gets a vote. Open K⁺ channels alone and the membrane drifts to EK, near −90 mV. Open Na⁺ channels alone and it drifts to ENa, near +60 mV. A real membrane has several channel types open at once, so it sits at a conductance-weighted average of their equilibrium potentials. That is where the resting potential and the action potential come from.

Driving force away from equilibrium

If other channels, or a voltage clamp, hold the membrane away from Eion, the net push on that ion is proportional to Vm − Eion, the driving force. By convention a positive value means outward current. For a cation the ions leave; for an anion the ions enter, because current counts charge rather than particles.

Words you will see

Membrane voltage, Vm
The voltage across the membrane, inside relative to outside. 0 mV means no charge has been separated yet.
Equilibrium potential, Eion
The one value of Vm at which an ion’s electrical push exactly cancels its diffusion push. Every ion has its own.
Nernst equation
The formula that computes Eion from the concentration ratio and the temperature.
Driving force, Vm − Eion
How far the membrane is from that ion’s equilibrium. Zero at Eion; the larger it is, the harder the net push.
Voltage clamp
An experimental setup whose amplifier holds Vm at whatever value you choose, no matter what the ions do.
Concentration gradient
A difference in concentration across the membrane. Diffusion pushes ions down it, from crowded to sparse.

Try this in class

Items 7 and 8 use the extra controls behind “Show more”.

  1. Before pressing Play, click Next ion five times. Which arrow grew? Which stayed put? Why?
  2. Raise outside K⁺ from 5 to 10 mM (hyperkalemia). Predict the sign of the shift in EK before you look, then watch the membrane re-settle.
  3. Make inside and outside equal. What is the electrical push at equilibrium? Where did the “battery” go?
  4. Switch to Na⁺. Its ratio is about the same size as potassium’s, but flipped. Why does the membrane go positive?
  5. Cl⁻ is an anion. Predict which way it moves at 0 mV and what sign the membrane ends up with, then check.
  6. Set Vm to −70 mV with the slider, press Hold (a typical resting potential), and compare the net push on K⁺ and on Na⁺. Which ion is farther from equilibrium at rest?
  7. Drop the temperature from 37 °C to 20 °C. The slope changes but the sign does not. Why?
  8. Choose Ca²⁺ and read the “how many ions” box. Why can a real cell never actually reach ECa?

About the model. Crossing rates follow a single-barrier (Eyring) rate model, so the two directions balance exactly at the Nernst potential. Vm follows the average of many random crossings, so it moves smoothly; Next ion shows the individual random events. The membrane capacitance is cartoon-scaled so that each ion visibly moves Vm. Concentrations are typical textbook values for a mammalian neuron.
