Vm holds still when the down-pulls and the up-pulls match. An ion’s pull grows with its number of open channels and with its distance from its own E.

The vote, as an equation

Each ion’s equilibrium potential is weighted by its conductance g, here the number of open channels. Textbooks also write this with permeabilities, the Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equation. Same idea: each ion’s vote counts in proportion to how easily it crosses.

What sets the resting potential?

The one-sentence version. The resting potential is the compromise voltage at which the pulls of all the ions that can cross the membrane cancel out, each pull weighted by how many channels that ion has. K⁺ has by far the most, so rest sits close to EK.

Every ion votes

In the first demo one ion had the membrane to itself, so Vm went to that ion’s equilibrium potential and stopped. A real membrane has several kinds of channel open at once. Each ion pulls Vm toward its own E, and two things set the strength of that pull: how many of its channels are open, and how far Vm is from its E. The membrane comes to rest where the pulls in the two directions match.

Why rest is not at EK

Na⁺ has only a few open channels at rest, but Vm is about 137 mV away from ENa, so even that small vote counts. It drags Vm a few millivolts above EK. Take the Na⁺ channel away and Vm falls exactly to EK; add Na⁺ channels and it climbs toward ENa. That climb is the beginning of the action potential.

Rest is a leak, not a lock

Because Vm is not at either ion’s equilibrium, K⁺ leaks out and Na⁺ leaks in the whole time the cell is “resting”. Left alone, those leaks would drain the gradients in a matter of hours and the resting potential would fade with them. The Na⁺/K⁺ pump prevents that: for each ATP it carries three Na⁺ out and two K⁺ in. Its main job is to maintain the gradients, and as a side effect it exports one net charge per cycle, which makes the inside a few millivolts more negative than the channels alone would. Holding the resting potential costs a sizeable share of a neuron’s energy budget.

Common mix-up. “The pump makes the resting potential.” Mostly not: the pump makes the gradients, and its direct electrical contribution is only a few millivolts. The resting potential itself comes from K⁺ leaking down the gradient the pump built. Switch the pump off here and Vm barely moves at first; it fades only as the gradients drain.

Steady state, not equilibrium

The single-ion membrane in the first demo reached a true equilibrium: nothing moved on net and no energy was needed. The resting membrane is a steady state instead. Vm is constant, but only because a continuous leak is matched by continuous pumping. That distinction is the reason the resting potential can be changed quickly: open more channels of one kind and the balance shifts at once.

What moves it

Two things change the resting potential. Opening more channels of one ion gives that ion more votes, so Vm slides toward its E. And changing a concentration moves that ion’s E, which moves Vm in proportion to the ion’s votes. Raising outside K⁺ is the clinical case: EK rises, Vm follows because K⁺ dominates, and excitable cells such as heart muscle misbehave.

Words you will see

Leak channel
A channel that is open at rest. Most of a neuron’s leak channels pass K⁺.
Conductance, g
How easily an ion crosses: more open channels, higher conductance. It is the weight of that ion’s vote.
Driving force, Vm − Eion
How far the membrane is from an ion’s equilibrium. Current through a channel is conductance times driving force.
Steady state
A voltage that holds still because opposite flows match, not because flow has stopped. Rest is a steady state; the first demo’s single-ion membrane was an equilibrium.
Na⁺/K⁺ pump
A transporter that uses ATP to move three Na⁺ out and two K⁺ in per cycle, maintaining both gradients.
Electrogenic
Moving net charge across the membrane. The pump is electrogenic because 3 out and 2 in leaves one charge unbalanced.
Hyperkalemia
High K⁺ in the blood. It raises EK, depolarizes resting cells, and can stop the heart.

Try this in class

  1. Set Vm to 0 mV with the slider and let go. Watch it settle back to rest. Which ion does most of the work?
  2. Remove the Na⁺ channel. Where does Vm go, and why exactly there?
  3. Add Na⁺ channels one at a time. How many before Vm crosses 0 mV? That is a preview of the action potential.
  4. Click the K⁺ channels closed one by one. What happens to the weight of the Na⁺ vote?
  5. Turn the pump off and wait. Which drains first, the gradients or the voltage? Why does resting cost ATP?
  6. Raise outside K⁺ from 5 to 10 mM. Predict the direction of the shift before you look. Why is high blood K⁺ dangerous for the heart?
  7. Drag a K⁺ ion to a Na⁺ channel, then to a K⁺ channel. What does “selective” mean?
  8. Add Cl⁻ channels until Cl⁻ has the most votes. Where does Vm settle, and what does that tell you about ECl?

About the model. Each open channel passes current in proportion to its driving force (the equivalent-circuit model used in electrophysiology), so the membrane settles at the conductance-weighted average of the equilibrium potentials, shifted a few millivolts by the pump’s current. Traffic through each channel splits into the two directions according to the same rule as the first demo, so each ion’s traffic balances exactly at its own Nernst potential. Vm follows the average of the crossings and moves smoothly. The number of ions on screen, the membrane capacitance and the speed at which the gradients drain with the pump off are cartoon-scaled; concentrations are typical textbook values for a mammalian neuron.
